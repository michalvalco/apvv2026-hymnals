## What the volume contains (per the review)

Structure: Hungarian and Czech prefaces → an introduction, *Forráskönyvek és szerzőik* ("Source books and their authors") → *Szerkesztési elvek* ("Editorial principles") → **the incipit index of 2,335 biblical-Czech or Slovak verse items drawn from 77 processed 17th-century prints** → a list of used and presumed sources → indexes.

**The introduction is in four parts**, and the division is itself an argument about how this literature arrived:

1. **Books of Bohemian origin** — six volumes first published in Bohemia. Two are 16th-century Czech translations of German devotional works — Johann Habermann's *Christliche Gebet…* and Urban Rhegius's *Seelenartzney…* — **which the Trenčín press took over and reissued**. Of the remaining four, two were published in Bohemia before White Mountain, one after, and one is inferred from content alone.
2. ⭐ **Exile literature (*exuláns irodalom*)** — verse appearing among refugees who left forcibly re-Catholicized Bohemia and Moravia after **White Mountain (1620)** and **largely integrated into the Hungarian Lutheran church**. Named: **Jiřík Třanovský**, whose *Písně duchovní staré y nové* the review calls "the most significant hymn collection of the Slavic Protestant community," transmitted across generations, part of both Slovak and Czech culture, and still in use in the 19th–20th centuries; and **Jan Amos Komenský**, who settled at Leszno, became bishop of the Czech Brethren emigrant congregations, and **taught at Sárospatak 1650–1654**.
3. **"Hungarus" authors** — the survival of those imported influences in locally-born writers. Named: **Štefan Pilárik**, of a multi-generational pastoral family, whose verse autobiography *Sors Pilarikiana* and prayer books are dominated by an unconditional attachment to the Kingdom of Hungary and the Lutheran church.
4. ⭐ **Presumed verses (*Feltételezett versek*)** — verse in lost or latent publications whose existence Hungarian book history only infers. **The review's worked example is two 17th-century editions of Třanovský's *Cithara Sanctorum*.**

## Key Arguments

1. **The repertory fills a structural gap in the Hungarian national bibliography, not a topical one.** *RMNy* indexes incipits **only for Hungarian-language verse**; verse in other languages was never indexed at all. Papp calls that restriction "rendszeridegen és szűkkeblű" — alien to the system and narrow-minded — in a retrospective national bibliography that otherwise treats the Carpathian Basin's linguistic diversity as a given. **This volume is the first correction of that, for the northern Slavic languages.**
2. **Incipits are given as two lines**, deliberately, to fix the difficulty caused by *RMNy*'s over-brief single-syntagma incipits, which can make different poems look identical.
3. **The corpus is print-only, and the author says so.** Manuscripts are excluded — self-limiting, and reasonable as a first systematic treatment. ⚠️ **Papp's caveat matters for TEPALIT:** in an early-modern culture where manuscript copying carried much of the circulation, a print-only count cannot establish actual diffusion or use. Cross-read with `Soltes_2025`, whose 19th-century sources are *manuscript* biblical-Czech.
4. **Czech-language printed verse begins in the Kingdom of Hungary with the Thirty Years' War period** — when, in Papp's framing, the local intellectual horizon fused with the culture, ecclesial habits and literary self-representation of the refugee population.
5. ⭐ **The scale comparison — Papp's own contribution, and directly usable.** 2,335 Czech/Slovak verse items from **77 prints**, set against roughly 3,500 items in the 17th-century *RMKT* series (possibly 5,000–6,000 on a different counting) from about **225 Hungarian-language prints**. **So the Hungarian corpus is roughly three times the Czech one** — a ratio that puts the Czech-language material well beyond "marginal" and gives TEPALIT a defensible sense of proportion it currently lacks.
6. ⚠️ **Neither corpus tells you about actual use.** Papp is explicit: neither *RPHA*, nor *RMKT*, nor this repertory lets you count the textological witnesses behind a given poem without days of manual work, and the datasets are not commensurable anyway (the Hungarian ones include manuscripts; this one does not).
7. **A network claim, offered as the payoff:** the study of these verse remains "sketched a network of connections that later had serious influence on the internal relations of Lower and Upper Hungary."

## Papp's criticisms — worth knowing before relying on it

- **No biographical apparatus.** The volume names many Bohemian refugee and locally-born authors **described in Hungarian scholarship for the first time**, but gives them no data section or index — no basic biographical data, and no marker distinguishing Bohemian-born from locally-born writers. Papp says such a section "would have been a great help." **For TEPALIT this is a live cost:** identifying who an author was will mean going outside the volume.
- ⭐ **It is a printed index, not a database, and that is the reviewer's main structural complaint.** "The continuation is fundamentally important, but given the quantity and nature of the material, alongside publication in volumes a suitable database would be necessary." Combined with point 6 above: **this book answers "does this incipit occur, and where," and cannot answer "how many witnesses" or "how did this spread" without manual labour.** Budget accordingly.
