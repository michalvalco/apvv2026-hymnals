## Summary

A progress report on a long catalogue project, and — for this chapter — the best available map of the exact linguistic territory the *Cithara Sanctorum* occupies.

The project began at István Käfer's initiative: an exhaustive collection of the **biblical-Czech verse** printed in the Kingdom of Hungary. The 17th-century phase is complete (2,335 items, published 2022). This article reports on extending it to the 18th century in two stages — 1701–1760 and 1761–1801 — with roughly 2,000 items processed so far, enough for a preliminary periodisation.

The 17th-century background it sketches is the confessional-migration story: after **White Mountain (8 November 1620)** and the *Obnovené zřízení zemské* (1627/1628), Czech Protestant intellectuals fled into Hungary, and their presence gave the period a heavy Czech stamp and a dominance of Protestant religious works. Most biblical-Czech works printed in Hungary have Czech antecedents — often new editions of works already popular in Bohemia, but **rarely reprinted without interpolation and change**.

The article's periodisation of the 18th century is the part the chapter can use directly. First half: a marked increase in Czech-language publication over the previous period; Evangelical dominance maintained, but Catholic publication also growing. Mid-century: the **first Reformed Slovak publications in the Eastern Slovak dialect** are printed in Debrecen, and **Czech influence begins to recede**; most new hymns are now written or translated by *Slovak* authors, with Hungarian and probably Polish hymn traditions joining Czech and German as sources. 1761–1801: the shortest but most productive stretch, dominated by small booklets carrying one or a few hymns, with a rise in secular material — biblical Czech still in use and still dominant, but increasingly shadowed by print in Slovak dialects.

## Key Arguments

1. **Biblical Czech (*bibličtina*) is a measurable corpus, not an atmosphere.** 2,335 items for the 17th century, ~2,000 more in progress for the 18th. The chapter's claims about Czech→Slovak Lutheran transfer can be set against counts rather than impressions.
2. **Reprinting is editing.** Czech works reissued in Hungary "rarely appeared without interpolations and changes." The article's worked example: the 1683 Levoča edition of Sixt Palma Močidlanský's *Perlička dítek Božích*, for which RMNy credits the Levoča editor **Daniel Sinapius as co-author** on account of the compilation and re-editing. **This is the chapter's "Lutheranisation" thesis, stated as a bibliographic norm rather than a special claim about the *Cithara*.**
3. **New hymnals were assembled by selection from older Czech collections** — including, explicitly, the *Cithara sanctorum*, whose hymns "are mostly of Czech origin," compiled by the Czech exile **Jiřík Třanovský**.
4. ⭐ **Language was worked as a confessional marker, deliberately.** The Slovak Catholic *Cantus catholici* draws largely on the hymnals of Jiřík Hlohovský, Jan Rozenplut and the *Český dekakord*, but its editor **Szöllősi Benedek adjusted the texts toward the West Slovak dialect specifically to demonstrate separation from the Protestant churches** — and even so, the result is better described as Slovakised Czech than as Slovak. **The confessional boundary was drawn in orthography and dialect, on a shared Czech textual inheritance.**
5. **Compilation could cross several source volumes.** *Korunka, aneb vínek panenský* was assembled by the press's owner Alžběta Dadanka from three earlier prints — a 1616 Prague book of the same title, a Kralice imprint (*Mravy ctnostné*), and the verse prayer-book *Přidavkové…* (Trenčín, 1641).
6. **The recession of Czech is datable, and it is mid-18th century.** Not a vague drift: it turns when Slovak-dialect printing begins in Debrecen and Slovak authorship of new hymns takes over.

## Notable Quotes

Page numbers are the journal's printed pagination (217–225). The p. 225 quotations are the **author's own published English**; the p. 218 quotation is Hungarian with a working rendering.

> "The first half of the century witnessed a significant increase in the number of Czech-language publications compared with the previous period, and although evangelical dominance was maintained, the number of Catholic publications also grew. In the mid-18th century, the first Reformed Slovak publications in the Eastern Slovak dialect were printed in Debrecen. At this point, Czech influence began to recede." (p. 225, English abstract)
- Context: **the citable periodisation.** Published English, no translation risk.

> "The biblical Czech language continued to be used, and even remained dominant, but more and more publications appeared in various dialects of Slovak." (p. 225, English abstract)
- Context: the terminus. Useful for placing the chapter's corpus on a trajectory rather than treating it as static.

> "The majority of the new hymns were written or translated by Slovak authors. In addition to Czech and German connections, the Hungarian and – presumably also – the Polish hymn traditions were important factors." (p. 225, English abstract)
- Context: ⚠️ **a caution as much as a finding.** A comparison framed purely as Czech↔Slovak Lutheran risks missing the Hungarian and Polish inputs the cataloguer can see. Note the author's own hedge on Polish ("presumably").

> "Az evangélikus énekeskönyv, a cseh exuláns, Jiřík Třanovský által összeállított Cithara sanctorum énekei is többségében cseh eredetűek." (p. 218)
- *Working rendering:* "The hymns of the Evangelical hymnal, the *Cithara sanctorum* compiled by the Czech exile Jiřík Třanovský, are likewise mostly of Czech origin."
- Context: the chapter's central object, placed by an external cataloguer inside a general pattern. ⚠️ Working rendering — verify before quoting.

> "…a szerkesztő, Szöllősi Benedek az énekszövegeket igyekezett a nyugatszlovák dialektushoz igazítani, hogy a protestáns egyházaktól való elkülönülést ezzel is demonstrálja… Ennek ellenére nyelve inkább mondható szlovakizált cseh nyelvnek, mint szlováknak." (p. 218)
- *Working rendering:* "…the editor, Benedek Szöllősi, sought to adjust the hymn texts to the West Slovak dialect, so as thereby to demonstrate separation from the Protestant churches… Despite this, its language is better called Slovakised Czech than Slovak."
- Context: ⭐ **the sharpest single point for the chapter.** Confessional identity enacted through dialect choice over a shared Czech inheritance — the Catholic mirror-image of the Lutheran retention the chapter analyses. ⚠️ Working rendering.

## Relation to TEPALIT Chapter

- **Which locus/section:** **§5 Transfer and reception** as the primary home, and **§2 Confessional context** for the White Mountain migration frame. Potentially **§1/§4** wherever the chapter needs to establish that adaptation-in-reprinting was the norm rather than the *Cithara*'s peculiarity.
- **How it supports our argument:** It converts the chapter's central claim from an interpretation into a documented regularity. "Czech material was received and re-framed in Slovak Lutheran use" is, on Kovács's evidence, simply how this print culture worked — Czech antecedents, edited on reissue, sometimes to the point of a new co-author being credited. The *Cithara* is then an instance of a pattern, which is a **stronger** position than treating it as sui generis, and it gives the chapter a corpus-scale backdrop (2,335 + ~2,000 items) it currently lacks.
- **Points of agreement:** Reprinting as editing; the exile-migration mechanism; the *Cithara*'s predominantly Czech textual stock; language as a confessional instrument.
- **Points of disagreement or tension:** (a) ⚠️ **The Hungarian and Polish inputs are a genuine complication for a bilateral Czech–Slovak frame.** The chapter should either address them or state explicitly that it brackets them. (b) This is **bibliography, not theology** — it counts and describes; it makes no claim about doctrinal content, and must not be enlisted for one. (c) The recession of Czech from the mid-18th century sits at the far edge of the chapter's window and may cut against a narrative of continuous transfer; better to name the turn than to end the story before it.
