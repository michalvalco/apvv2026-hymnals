## Summary

A conservation find, reported as a piece of detective work. During the restoration of a **1614 Wittenberg Latin homiletic volume** (Fridericus Balduin, *Hypomnēmata Homiliarum In Epistolas Dominicales*) held in the old collection of the Tessedik Sámuel Museum and Town Library in **Szarvas**, printed fragments in Czech and German were recovered **from the binding**. The Czech portions turned out to be partly prose, partly notated hymns.

The prose sections came from a hymnal's preface, and the preface carried the clue that identified it: it refers back to the hymnal of **Jakub Kunvaldský**, a Lutheran pastor active in Moravia (Přerov, Starý Jičín), and states that Kunvaldský's book had appeared **25 years earlier**. That dating placed the fragment's parent volume in the first years of the seventeenth century, and comparison established that the Szarvas text agrees almost entirely with **Tobiáš Závorka's** hymnal — the article works with *Písně chval božských* (1602 / 1606 editions), and the fragment now carries the shelfmark of the 1602 edition.

The significance is not the hymnal, which is known, but **where the fragment was found and what that implies**. A Czech-language Lutheran hymnal was cut up and used as binding waste for a Latin Wittenberg homiletic volume, and the resulting book ended up in a **Slovak Lutheran town's collection**. The article thus documents a concrete, physical instance of Czech hymnodic material circulating in, and being consumed by, the Lutheran book culture of the Kingdom of Hungary.

Kunvaldský matters as background: he was the first to compile a hymnal for the Moravian Lutherans who had separated from the Unity of Brethren, drawing on old church folk hymns and his own compositions, and deliberately aiming at "uneducated, simple people" — plain structures, plain melodies, sometimes rough text, with melody references frequently pointing to Moravian folk songs.

## Key Arguments

1. **The identification is built on an internal cross-reference, not on a title page.** The fragment preserves no title; it is identified by its preface's own back-reference to Kunvaldský's hymnal "25 years" earlier, which brackets the date, and then by textual comparison against Závorka. The method is worth noting because the chapter faces the same problem with the *Cithara Sanctorum*'s lost 1636 first edition, where **no complete copy survives** — identification through apparatus rather than through the object.
2. **Binding waste is evidence of circulation and of obsolescence at once.** A book has to be both present and considered expendable to end up in a binding. That is a different and in some ways stronger claim about reception than a surviving library copy: it shows the hymnal was in the region and had been superseded.
3. **The Moravian Lutheran hymnal tradition is a distinct strand**, separate from the Unity of Brethren, and Kunvaldský is its starting point. The article's footnote on him lists three Olomouc imprints (1572, 1576, 1576) with Knihopis identifiers.
4. **Szarvas as find-site is not incidental.** A Slovak Lutheran town in the southern Hungarian plain holding a Wittenberg volume bound with Czech hymnal waste is the transfer route made material.

## Relation to TEPALIT Chapter

- **Which locus/section:** **§5 Transfer and reception** primarily — this is the chapter's own thesis in miniature, evidenced physically. Secondarily **§2 Confessional context**, for the Moravian-Lutheran/Brethren distinction that Kunvaldský's career marks.
- **How it supports our argument:** The chapter argues comparison "through reception" — Slovak Lutheran sources adapting Czech Reformation material. This article supplies a **material instance** rather than a textual one: Czech Lutheran hymnody physically present in a Hungarian-Kingdom Lutheran book, and present as *waste*, which dates its supersession. Reception is usually argued from textual borrowing; here it can be pointed at.
- **Points of agreement:** The Moravian Lutheran hymnal tradition as distinct from the Brethren's; the pastoral, deliberately plain register of that tradition; the assumption that Czech-language hymnody circulated as a matter of course in the region.
- **Points of disagreement or tension:** None substantive — this is descriptive bibliography, not an interpretive rival. The real tension is **evidential reach**: one binding fragment in one town supports a claim about *circulation*, not about *use in worship*. Do not let it carry more than that. And the chapter should be careful not to fold Závorka (Czech Lutheran, Moravia) into the Brethren line; the article's own framing keeps them apart, and so should we.
