## Summary

A discourse analysis of how Catholics and Lutherans in Upper Hungary came to see themselves and each other between the 1830s and the 1850s, built from the contemporary press together with printed **and manuscript** homiletic and catechetical literature.

The context is a confessional conflict entangled with a political one: the effort to put Protestantism on legal and social parity with Catholicism produced friction from the 1830s, and that friction ran along the fault line between liberal Hungarian nationalism and a conservative, Vienna-loyal clerical-bureaucratic order. Confessional stratification mapped substantially onto ethnic difference — Hungarians, Slovaks, Germans — which made religious affiliation both an obstacle to national homogenisation and an instrument of political mobilisation, most visibly in 1848/9 and under the neo-absolutist interventions that followed (the 1855 Concordat, the 1859 Protestant Edict).

Upper Hungary (*Hungaria superior*) is the site because it was the institutional and spiritual centre of Hungarian Lutheranism: three of the four Evangelical districts had their seats there, along with the grammar schools and lyceums that formed the elite. Šoltés gives Gottas's pre-1848 estimate of ~830,000 Lutherans in the Kingdom — **450,000 of them Slovaks**, 200,000 Germans, 180,000 Hungarians.

The analysis contrasts the two confessional discourses through their catechisms. Catholic discourse emphasised dogmatic integrity, Church authority, and the exclusivity of salvation within the institutional church. Evangelical catechisms, all built on Luther's Small Catechism but pedagogically updated, were fundamentally critical of Catholicism yet **did not build a strictly exclusive soteriology**: personal faith in Christ sufficed for salvation regardless of formal affiliation, and the church was a spiritual community of believers rather than a necessary administrator of grace. Karol Kuzmány's *Katechizmus evanjelický* (1845) and *Príprava konfirmantov* (1843) became the formative texts of Slovak Evangelical confessional identity into the twentieth century, and Šoltés reads Kuzmány — and Ján Šulek before him — as strongly marked by theological rationalism.

The conclusion is that the second confessionalization did not replicate early-modern disciplinary mechanisms but was a distinct process of **symbolic identity consolidation under the conditions of modernity**, in which the decisive site was local — schools, parishes, sermons, confraternities, instructional texts.

## Key Arguments

1. **"Second confessionalization" is a period concept and it is not the first one.** Šoltés is explicit that the 19th-century process differs in kind from early-modern confessionalization. **This is the sentence that governs how the chapter may use him — as contrast, or as terminus, never as parallel evidence.**
2. **Confession and ethnicity were substantially co-extensive in Upper Hungary**, which made confessional discourse simultaneously national discourse. More than half of the Kingdom's Lutherans were Slovaks.
3. **Catholic and Evangelical apologetics diverged in structure, not merely in content:** Catholic on institutional authority and exclusive salvation; Evangelical on biblicality, rationality, freedom of conscience, and a supra-confessional understanding of Christ's Church.
4. **A "threatened minority" narrative consolidated on the Evangelical side**, rooted in the collective memory of pre-toleration persecution — carried not only in journalism but in **manuscript sermons and religious manuals**.
5. **The catechism is the unit of analysis.** Confessional identity is traced through what was actually put in front of children, which is a method the chapter can borrow even where the period does not transfer.
6. **Theological rationalism reshaped Slovak Lutheran catechesis by the 1840s** — Kuzmány declines to say which visible church is the saving one, positing instead one general, holy, eternal Church of Christ standing above all visible and imperfect churches.

## Relation to TEPALIT Chapter

- **Which locus/section:** **§2 Confessional context only**, and explicitly as *terminus* rather than background — where the chapter wants to gesture at what the transfer it documents eventually became. **Not corpus. Not comparative evidence.**
- ⭐ **The one reason it earns a place here at all, and it is a real one.** Šoltés's manuscript sources are Slovak Lutheran homiletic and apologetic texts from the 1830s–50s held in the **Slovak National Library Literary Archive** — and their titles are in **biblical Czech**: *Obrana Cjrkwe a náboženstwj Kristowa* (1838), *Mámli gá Ewangelíkem gen porád zůstati? Aneb Nábožobné Rozmlauwánj Swětomila z Bohuslawem* (1838), *Wystraha pred Manželstwjm rozdjlného Naboženstwj* (1817), and Daniel Kicska's sermons (1855). **That is the same *bibličtina* register as the *Cithara Sanctorum*, still doing confessional work in manuscript two hundred years later.** The chapter traces a transfer; this article, without setting out to, shows where that transfer ended up. Cross-read with `Kovacs_2025_BiblikusCseh`, which dates the *print* recession of biblical Czech to the mid-18th century — **Šoltés's manuscripts suggest the manuscript life of the register outlasted its print life**, which is a genuinely interesting observation and, as far as this note can tell, nobody's stated claim.
- **How it supports our argument:** indirectly and at one remove. It does not evidence anything about the 16th–18th centuries. It shows the durability of the linguistic and confessional formation the chapter analyses.
- **Points of disagreement or tension:** ⚠️ **The period gap is the whole tension and it must not be smoothed.** Šoltés's own conclusion forbids treating his process as continuous with early-modern confessionalization. Any use here has to say "two centuries later" out loud. There is also a **scope temptation to resist**: the article is genuinely excellent and directly about Slovak Lutherans, which makes it easy to over-cite in a chapter it does not actually serve.
